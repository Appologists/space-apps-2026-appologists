## Scaffold defaults, for the team to confirm or change

These were chosen to get a working skeleton. None is settled until the team says so.

| # | Default | Why | If you disagree |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | **Compute Sun and Earth positions in the browser** (Astronomy Engine) and use JPL Horizons as the check, not as the live source. The kickoff plan was to call Horizons directly. | Ranking 23 sites over 14 days needs about 30,000 positions; Horizons answers one site and one body per request and cannot be called from a browser without a proxy. Our numbers agree with Horizons to 0.004°. | `src/astro/horizons.js` already talks to Horizons; a criterion could use it instead, at the cost of speed. |
| B | **Vite, plain JavaScript modules, three.js, Vitest.** No UI framework. | Few moving parts; every AI tool and teammate can read it. | React or similar can be added without touching `src/astro`, `src/criteria` or `src/scoring`. |
| C | **Score = weighted average of 0–1 scores. Missing data is left out, never counted as zero.** | Honest about what we do not know. | Change `rankSites` in `src/scoring/rank.js`. |
| D | **"Sun up" means the centre of the Sun's disc is above the horizon**, lowered a little for panel height. | Simple and standard. The Sun's disc is 0.53° wide, which matters at the poles. | `minSunElevationDeg` in `src/mission.js`. |
| E | **Default mission: lands now, lasts 14 Earth days, panel and antenna at 2 m.** | Placeholders. | `defaultMission` in `src/mission.js`. |
| F | **The 2.6 MB Moon map stays in git.** | The app must start after `npm install` with nothing else to download. | Move it to the catalog. |
| G | **Terrain is served on demand by the dev server**, as a polar grid around the point: 720 compass directions, about 660 distances out to 300 km. | Lets you stand anywhere the data covers without preparing files first, and the same samples draw the ground and give the horizon. | Needs `npm run dev` and the 115 MB download. For a hosted demo, precompute patches for chosen sites into `data/derived/`. |
| H | **In the surface view, ground in shadow is drawn dark grey, not black.** | So you can see its shape. Real shadowed ground gets only faint light bounced from sunlit slopes. | `SHADOW_FILL` in `src/surface/surface-view.js`. |
| Z | **Landing ground is ranked by room to miss, not by a 0–1 score (2026-11-14, Daniel).** Room to miss is the distance in metres from a spot to the nearest ground that fails a check on its own (too steep, no Sun or no Earth at touchdown, dark too long, or no terrain data). The listed sites are the passing spots with the most room, spaced apart as before; equal room goes to the shorter longest darkness, then to the gentler landing area (the order of the tie-breaks is our choice). On the map, landing ground is a lighter blue where it has more room, from the landing accuracy to the most room in that search. The search question no longer carries weights. This replaces the scoring half of decision K; pass or fail is unchanged. | The score (0.59 × longest unbroken sunlight as a share of the mission + 0.41 × share of time with Earth in view) barely varied: at Peak near Shackleton more than half the passing ground is sunlit for all 14 days and Earth is in view 59–71% of the time everywhere, so the median was 0.868, the best 0.881 and the ten listed sites differed by 0.004. It re-measured what the pass rules had already demanded. Room to miss is the landing accuracy at which a spot would stop passing, so it needs no weights and ties to the 100 m, 200 m and 2 km choices; there it runs from 113 to 466 m, and two of the old top ten had the least room of all. | `roomToMiss`, `pickSites` in `src/finder/score.js`; the `room` raster and `roomM` of each site (`src/finder/types.js`); `roomRange` in `src/finder/map-layers.js`. It is measured between cell centres (80 or 100 m), so it moves in steps of about a cell and knows nothing of rocks or craters smaller than one; ground beyond the edge of the searched square counts as failing. It does not look at other landing dates or at what the rover can reach. Kept answers are searched afresh after this change: run `npm run demo:warm` again. |
| Y | **The Drive step's map shows sunlight, slope and landing ground together (2026-11-14, Daniel).** A fourth choice on the map's switch, "All", is what the Drive step opens on. Brightness is sunlight at the time shown (the landing hour until the drive is played); colour marks steep ground in the route's colours at full strength, and ground under 10° is left a plain grey; landing ground is washed blue with a bright outline. Day/night, Slope and Landing sites stay on the switch, each showing one of the three alone. | While picking a start and destinations the presenter could see only one of the three. Light and dark is how people already read day and night, colour is kept for what to avoid, and an outline costs no colour. No shadowed ground is as bright as any sunlit ground (tested), so brightness always means sunlight. | `allPixel`, `allPictures`, `composeAll` in `src/finder/map-layers.js`; `MAP_VIEWS` in `src/ui/flow.js`. It sets aside decision J for this view (not one hue, not flat) and decision T's green for ground under 10° on the map (the route keeps it). The picture is drawn three times finer than the search's cells, with the shading and slope colours smoothed between cells, a drawing aid the legend states; sunlight and landing ground keep the cells' edges. Earth in view is not on it (it is in the landing check and the hover line). |
| X | **The mission regions' sunlight and Earth maps are computed in advance (2026-11-14, Daniel).** Hourly, from 14 Nov 2026 to 15 Jan 2027, by `npm run data:light`, into `data/raw/light/` (about 350 MB, not in git). A search reads them when everything they depend on matches; otherwise it computes them as before. | The shadow sweep is nearly all of a search's time, and it does not depend on the landing limits, so with fixed regions it can be done once. A search there then takes about a second instead of 10 s (20 s at 20 km), for any limits or whole-hour landing time in the stretch. Stored and computed answers are the same number for number (`npm run check:light`). | `server/light-store.mjs`, `tools/precompute-light.mjs`, `lightMaps` in `server/landing-search.mjs`. |
| W | **The five crater regions are searched 20 km round their centres; the two NASA-region sites stay at 10 km (2026-11-14, Daniel).** The radius is set per region in `data/missions.json` (`searchRadiusKm`, our choice). At 20 km the search uses 100 m cells. | A crater's centre is its floor: dark, and often without Earth in view, so a 10 km search round it found nothing. At 20 km it reaches the rims. On 21 Nov 2026 (14 days, Pre-set limits): Shackleton 5.0 km², de Gerlache 5.7 km², Malapert 3.4 km² can take a landing; Haworth and Nobile still none (darkness). Each search takes 10–12 s. A 20 km circle round Shackleton's centre also takes in ground near Connecting Ridge and Peak near Shackleton. | `searchQuestion` in `src/finder/client.js`; `tools/warm-demo.mjs` asks the same. |
| V | **Landing accuracy choices: 100 m, 200 m, 2 km (2026-11-14, Daniel).** 100 m is NASA's figure for the Artemis lander (hls-2024 in `data/missions.json`); 200 m and 2 km are our choices, 2 km standing for an older-style lander. 50 m was dropped. The rule stays: accuracy is a circle round the site, and every 80 m cell inside it must pass. | 50 m and 100 m gave identical results (the search rounds the radius to whole 80 m cells). Measured on the seven demo regions (21 Nov 2026, 14 days, slopes up to 10°): 100 m finds 8.4 km² that can take a landing, 200 m 1.8 km², 300 m 0.7 km², 500 m and 2 km none (every circle holds ground steeper than 10°). So the three choices show precise landing opening up the pole, step by step. | `GROUPS` in `src/ui/flow.js`; the search in `server/landing-search.mjs` (cap 30 cells, 2.4 km). |
| U | **A drive succeeds when the rover reaches its last destination (2026-11-14, Daniel).** Darkness on the way still counts against what the rover survives; darkness at the last destination after it arrives does not, and playback ends at the arrival. This replaces decision O's "not lost there before the mission ends". The old rule stays available as `surviveAfterArrival: true` on the rover (tested both ways against the exhaustive search). | A demo drive is about getting there; a destination that goes dark later (one at Connecting Ridge is sunlit only until day 2.5) made a good drive look like a failure. | `simulatePlan` in `src/finder/schedule.js`, `solve` in `src/finder/planner.js`, `playEnd` in `src/ui/flow.js`. |
| T | **The map's Slope view uses the route's four slope bands (2026-11-14, Daniel).** Under 10° green, 10–15° yellow, 15–20° orange, over 20° red, the same key as the route; the relief shading changes only brightness. This replaces decision J's one-hue ramp for the finder map's Slope layer only (the surface view's lenses keep J). The map is coloured from a small panel on the map itself: Day/night, Slope, Landing sites. | One key for the ground and the route, so a judge can see why the route bends. The four colours step down in lightness from green to red, so they stay apart without red–green vision. | `src/finder/slope-bands.js`, `layerPixel` in `src/finder/map-layers.js`, the toggle in `src/ui/flow.js`. |
| S | **One drive player (2026-11-14, Daniel).** "Play the drive" is the only way to play a drive back. Khiem's hour-by-hour story (PR #7) is folded into it: a bar for the unbroken time in the dark against what the rover survives, one sentence on what the rover is doing and why, and "Next change", which jumps to the next time it starts doing something else. His route cards, slope-coloured route, why-not box, height profile and directions stay; the profile and directions are folded so Play is on screen without scrolling. | Two players on one map gave two rovers at two different times, and only one of them could drive the Ride step. One clock keeps the map, the time bar and the surface view together. | `src/ui/flow.js` (the player), `src/finder/playback.js` (time in the dark, changes), `STATE_STORY` in `src/finder/plan-text.js`. |
| R | **Route choices: safest, fastest, shortest (2026-11-14, proposed by Khiem, our estimate for the numbers).** Fastest is the drive planner as it is. Safest is the planner run again with stricter limits, strictest first: slopes up to 10°, then 12°, then the rover's own limit, each first with 30% of the darkness allowance held back (50 h → 35 h) and then without; if the fastest plan already keeps to the limits found with less darkness, it is used instead. Shortest is the shortest drivable path driven as soon as the rules allow, shown even when the rover is lost, to show why the planner waits and goes round. Routes are coloured by slope: under 10°, 10–15°, 15–20°, over 20°. | The planner goes for the earliest arrival and can use the whole darkness allowance (plan item 14). Re-running it with stricter limits gives a safer plan without touching the search. 10° matches the finder's landing-slope default and 15° the rover's; 20° and the 30% margin are our estimate, not from a source. | `src/finder/route-options.js` (`SAFE_SLOPES_DEG`, `DARK_MARGIN`, `SLOPE_BANDS`). Safest is not the safest possible plan: it is the planner's plan under tighter limits. |
| Q | **"More tools" is off the page unless the address has `?tools` (2026-11-14, Daniel).** That is the ranked list of known sites, the weights, the terrain data picker, the exact-number form and the site card with the JPL Horizons check. Nothing is deleted. | The demo is the five steps; anything else on screen is something to explain in 120 seconds. | `src/main.js` (one line), `index.html`. The ranked list still assumes a smooth Moon, another reason to keep it out of the demo. |
| P | **The app opens as five mission steps (2026-11-14).** Mission, Regions, Drive, Surface, Ride, each with its own view; the ranked list, weights, terrain data and exact-number form move under "More tools" on the first step. A mission profile's numbers each name a source in `data/missions.json`, or say "our choice". The profiles land on 21 November 2026, 00:00 UTC. The regions are a fixed list of seven points, each searched 10 km around. The dev server keeps every search answer, in memory and in `node_modules/.cache/landing-search`, under a key made of the question, the terrain data it can read and the dates of the search code. | The demo is 120 seconds, so one path with big controls and a Next button; and a planner's real question runs in that order. The date is our choice because on the hackathon's own dates Earth is below the horizon at the south pole and no landing that needs Earth passes; our scan of landing dates found 17 to 29 November passing and 1 to 13 December not. NASA publishes names but no coordinates for its Artemis III candidate regions, so "Connecting Ridge" and "Peak near Shackleton" use sites proposed inside them by Gracy and Lee (LPSC 2024, abstract 1695) and the other five are named crater centres, which mostly fail and say why. Kept answers make a reload or a step back instant; the key means a changed question, new terrain data or changed search code is searched afresh. | `src/ui/flow.js`, `data/missions.json`, `server/terrain-plugin.mjs`. During playback the rover's place between two time steps is a straight blend of the planner's replay, a drawing aid only (`src/finder/playback.js`). In the ride the Sun and Earth stay where they are at the landing spot (under 0.2° off over a few kilometres), and the app says so. |
| O | **Terrain is read as fine as the sample needs, no finer.** Callers of `heightAt`/`sampleAt` may say how many metres per pixel they need. "Best available" then uses the coarsest downloaded whole-Moon dataset that is still that fine, and falls back to finer, then coarser, where it has no data. The surface view asks for the gap between its rings (59 m data out to about 47 km, 470 m data beyond); the finder asks for its cell size (80–200 m for the search area, 500 m for the far grid). A regional grid (the south pole at 80 m) is always first where it covers. | The 59 m dataset is 34 GB. Far ground is sampled every 500–1000 m, so 59 m data adds nothing there and reading it would touch gigabytes: at Apollo 11 a view reads 6 MB this way, against 100 MB when the 59 m data is chosen by itself. The polar grid is made for the pole, where a whole-Moon map's pixels turn to slivers. | `server/composite.mjs`. The cost: slope far from the viewer is measured over a bigger pixel, and heights can step by a few metres where the dataset changes; the app's notes say far ground comes from coarser data. Checked 2026-11-14 on all 16 tiles against the 470 m map, 3,000 points per tile: correlation 0.9998 to 1.0000, mean difference at most 2.1 m, spread 13–23 m. The four southern tiles against the 80 m south-pole grid at 80–88° S: correlation 1.0000, mean difference at most 0.1 m, spread 1.8–3.2 m. |
| O | **Drive planner rules (2026-11-14; replaces the fixed route of N, keeps its rules and tightens one).** The planner chooses the route, the order of several destinations and the waiting together, for the earliest arrival; among plans arriving in the same hour it takes the shorter drive. The rules are N's, with the sunlight check made at every cell the rover reaches, not only on the hour: it leaves a spot only while that spot is sunlit (and sees Earth, if required). So it can no longer coast across a dark cell inside one hour. It may wait in sunlight on purpose, and may set off part-way through an hour. It stays at the last destination, and the plan counts only if it is not lost there before the mission ends; darkness still going at the end of the mission is taken at its full length, so arriving just before the end does not count as surviving. | A fixed shortest path often runs into shadow where waiting a little earlier, or going round, gets there sooner or at all. Checking at every cell is the plain reading of "drives only in sunlight" and does not depend on where the hour happens to fall. Every plan is replayed by the same step-by-step rules before it is shown. | `src/finder/planner.js` (the search), `src/finder/schedule.js` (the rules and the replay). Checked against an exhaustive search on 8,000 small random maps: it found a plan whenever one existed and always arrived as early. Limits are listed under plan item 14. |
| N | **Timed drive rules.** The rover drives only while its spot is sunlit (and, if asked, in view of Earth); otherwise it waits in place. If it sits in darkness for longer than it survives, it is lost. Time moves in the search's steps (an hour for missions up to 14 days). The route itself is fixed: the shortest drivable path. | Simple enough to check by hand and to redo instantly; it answers "when should we leave, and do we make it?" | `src/finder/schedule.js`. It does not look for a different route that avoids shadow; that is the full version (plan item 14). |
| M | **Terrain data is chosen by the person using the app.** Datasets are downloaded one by one; the app lists what it can read and what is present. The default, "Best available", takes the finest dataset covering the spot and fills in beyond its edges from a whole-Moon one. | Datasets run from 66 MB to tens of GB, so nobody should be forced to download them all, and results should say which data they came from. | `server/terrain-sources.mjs`. Checked 2026-11-14: the whole-Moon 1.9 km file matches the south-pole 80 m file at 3,000 points (correlation 1.000, mean difference 1 m, spread 54 m). |
| L | **Route rules.** A cell is drivable if the terrain slope there is within the rover's limit; the route is the shortest path over drivable cells (steps to the eight neighbours, no cutting corners), measured along the ground including height changes. Driving time is distance divided by an average speed. | Simple, fast enough to redo on every change, and conservative: the terrain's steepest direction is used whichever way the rover crosses it. | `src/finder/route.js`. It does not prefer gentler or sunnier ground, and it ignores time; see plan item 14. |
| K | **Landing-site finder rules.** A site passes only if every point within the landing accuracy passes every check. "Sunlit" and "Earth in view" mean the centre of the disc clears the terrain, seen from panel or antenna height. Passing ground is scored with the same two weights as the ranked list. Found sites are listed with the finder, not mixed into the ranked list. | The lander may come down anywhere in its ellipse. One score rule keeps the two views comparable. The ranked list still assumes a smooth Moon, so mixing the two sets of numbers would mislead. | `src/finder/score.js`, `server/landing-search.mjs`. Defaults (500 m, 10°, 50 h) are placeholders. |
| J | **Lens colours follow fixed rules**: one hue per lens running dark (low) to light (high), blue for elevation and orange for slope, no rainbow; drawn flat with no sunlight, shadow or grain on top; ground without data is neutral grey. Shape comes from height contour lines and distance rings instead. | In a lens, brightness is the measurement, so nothing else may change it. One hue is readable by colour-blind viewers and in a dark scene. Both ramps step through the same lightness values (tested). | `src/surface/lenses.js`. |
| I | **The surface view's ground has decorative grain** (generated in the shader, no image file), and flat sunlit ground is drawn mid-grey. | Without it the ground was a blank white sheet. Every grain scale is smaller than the data's 80 m pixels, so nothing the data could show is invented, and the app says the grain is decoration. | `src/surface/ground-grain.js`; `EXPOSURE` in `src/surface/surface-view.js`. A real photo texture or LROC imagery would be the next step up. |
